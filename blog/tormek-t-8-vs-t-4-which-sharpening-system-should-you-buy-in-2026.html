Ask any woodworker or serious home cook which wet grinder to buy and you'll get the same two answers within thirty seconds: the Tormek T-8 or the smaller, cheaper Tormek T-4. They share the same jig ecosystem, the same water-cooled grinding philosophy and the same Swedish build reputation — but they are not the same machine, and one of them is genuinely wrong for a lot of buyers. The T-8 runs a 250 mm wheel and a continuous-duty motor; the T-4 runs a 200 mm wheel with a 30-minute duty cycle and weighs about half as much.

The other thing worth saying up front: Tormek is not the only credible option in 2026, and for a chunk of buyers it's not even the sensible one. If your entire sharpening life is six kitchen knives and a pair of scissors, spending $900 on a Swedish wet grinder is a hobby purchase, not a tool purchase. The Work Sharp Ken Onion Edition does that job for around $150. If you obsess over repeatable, measured bevel angles on folding knives, a guided-rod system like the Edge Pro Apex beats both Tormeks on precision per dollar. And in the shop, Jet's JSSG-10 undercuts the T-8 significantly while using Tormek-compatible jigs.

When buying a Tormek T sharpening system, verify that your knife collection includes primarily European-style blades (20-degree edges) rather than Asian knives (15-degree edges), since the machine's preset angles work best with standard Western knives and may require adjustment for other blade types.

Tormek T-8 — the shop workhorse

The T-8 is the machine most Tormek owners eventually end up with. It uses a 250 mm (10") SG-250 grindstone, a 220 mm leather honing wheel, and a motor rated for continuous running, so you can sharpen a full set of chisels, plane irons and turning gouges in one session without stopping to let it cool. The zinc-cast top with cast-iron universal support housing is the practical upgrade over older models: the EzyLock shaft means no tools to change wheels, and the support bar sits rigidly enough that jig repeatability is genuinely tight. Street price in 2026 sits around $850–$950 for the base machine, and that's before jigs — the SVM-45 knife jig, SVD-186 gouge jig and TTS-100 turning tool setter are separate purchases that can easily add $250+. You can check current bundle pricing and the accessory range direct at Tormek. What you're really buying is the jig ecosystem and the resale value — used T-8s hold price remarkably well.

Tormek T-4 — the kitchen and small-shop version

The T-4 costs roughly $500–$580 and takes the same jigs, the same universal support diameter and the same water-cooled approach. Differences that matter: a 200 mm wheel (smaller wheel = slightly more hollow grind on flat tools like plane irons, and a smaller effective surface), a plastic-topped housing instead of zinc, a 145 mm honing wheel, a smaller water trough that needs refilling more often, and a duty cycle of about 30 minutes on, 30 minutes off. For kitchen knives, pocket knives, scissors and the occasional chisel, that duty cycle is a non-issue — you'll be done in fifteen minutes. For a turner grinding a dozen bowl gouges on a Saturday, it's a real constraint. At around 7 kg it's also genuinely portable, which the 14.5 kg T-8 is not.

Work Sharp Ken Onion Edition — the value alternative for knives only

At roughly $150–$180, the Ken Onion Edition is a belt sharpener, not a wet grinder. Variable speed, adjustable guide from 15° to 30°, and it will take a dull kitchen knife to shaving sharp in about two minutes — faster than any Tormek. The catches are real: it's a dry grinder, so you can overheat thin edges if you linger, belts are consumables (a few dollars each, and you'll go through them), and it doesn't do woodworking tools well at all. The optional Blade Grinding Attachment adds convex-edge capability for another $80-ish. If your list of things to sharpen is entirely knives, this outperforms a T-4 on speed and on cost by a factor of three, and loses on edge consistency and long-term precision.

Edge Pro Apex — the precision guided-rod system

The Apex 4 kit runs about $300–$350 and takes a completely different approach: a fixed-angle guide rod with hand-held water stones. No motor, no heat, and angle repeatability that's arguably better than a Tormek knife jig because you're setting a measured degree rather than trusting a projection calculation. It's slow — a badly damaged edge can take 20 minutes of hand work — and it only does knives, plus it struggles with very long blades and heavy cleavers. Knife enthusiasts who mirror-polish edges tend to prefer it. Woodworkers have essentially no use for it.

Jet JSSG-10 — the direct T-8 competitor

Jet's wet sharpener runs a 10" wheel, a 1/5 HP motor and sells for roughly $500–$600, well under the T-8. Crucially, its universal support bar is 12 mm like Tormek's, so most Tormek jigs fit — a lot of owners buy the Jet machine and Tormek jigs deliberately. The trade-offs are build quality and refinement: the Jet's water trough and housing feel cruder, the stone that ships with it is generally rated below Tormek's SG grindstone, and the honing wheel setup is less polished. Jet's warranty support in Europe is also patchier than Tormek's. It's a legitimate 80%-of-the-machine-for-65%-of-the-price argument if you don't mind the finish.

Pros & Cons

Pros
  • ✓ Both Tormeks share one jig ecosystem — buy a knife or gouge jig once and it fits the T-4, T-8 and even the Jet JSSG-10, unlike Work Sharp or Edge Pro accessories which are locked to their own platform
  • ✓ Water-cooled grinding removes any risk of drawing the temper on thin kitchen blades — a genuine advantage over the dry-belt Work Sharp Ken Onion Edition
  • ✓ The T-8's continuous-duty motor lets you sharpen an entire chisel and gouge set in one sitting; the T-4 and most sub-$300 machines need cooling breaks
  • ✓ Tormek machines hold resale value far better than Jet or Work Sharp units — used T-8s routinely fetch 60–70% of new price years later
Cons
  • ✗ Both Tormeks are dramatically slower than a Work Sharp Ken Onion for basic kitchen knife touch-ups, and the T-4 costs three times as much for that job
  • ✗ The advertised price is misleading — a T-8 plus the knife, chisel and gouge jigs realistically lands near $1,200, while the Jet JSSG-10 delivers a comparable wheel size for hundreds less

Our Verdict

There isn't one winner here, there are four right answers to four different questions. If you sharpen woodworking tools regularly — chisels, plane irons, turning gouges, carving tools — buy the Tormek T-8; the continuous duty cycle, the larger wheel and the jig range justify the money and you'll never outgrow it. If you sharpen mostly kitchen knives but also want the option of doing shop tools properly, and you don't need to run for hours, the T-4 is the smarter buy at roughly $400 less — the duty cycle limit genuinely won't bother you. If knives are the only thing you'll ever sharpen and you want speed, buy the Work Sharp Ken Onion Edition and put the $400 you saved into better knives; just be disciplined about heat. If you're a knife obsessive chasing measured, repeatable bevel angles and mirror finishes, the Edge Pro Apex beats every powered option on precision per dollar. And if you want T-8 capability on a tighter budget and can live with rougher fit and finish, the Jet JSSG-10 plus Tormek jigs is the value play most people overlook.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Tormek jigs fit the T-4 and the Jet JSSG-10?

Yes. The T-4 and T-8 use the same 12 mm universal support bar, so jigs are fully interchangeable between them. The Jet JSSG-10 also uses a 12 mm support, and most Tormek jigs fit it — though a few accessories that mount to the machine housing rather than the support bar may not.

Is the T-4's 30-minute duty cycle actually a problem?

Only if you sharpen in long sessions. Doing a block of kitchen knives or a couple of chisels takes well under 30 minutes. It becomes limiting if you're reshaping badly damaged edges or grinding a full set of turning tools, which is exactly when the T-8's continuous-duty motor earns its price.

Will a Work Sharp damage my expensive kitchen knives?

It can if you're careless. It's a dry belt grinder, so heat build-up on thin, hard blades is a real risk if you dwell in one spot. Used with light pressure and steady passes it's fine, but a water-cooled Tormek removes that risk entirely — which is why many people with high-end Japanese knives pay the premium.

What does a Tormek T-8 actually cost once you add jigs?

The machine alone is roughly $850–$950 in 2026. Realistically add $80–$130 for a knife jig, similar for a square-edge jig, and more for gouge or scissor jigs — most owners spend $1,100–$1,300 in total. Budget for jigs before comparing headline prices against the Jet or Work Sharp.
