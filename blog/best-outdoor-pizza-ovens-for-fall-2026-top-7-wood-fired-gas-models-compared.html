Best Outdoor Pizza Ovens for Fall 2026: Top 7 Wood-Fired & Gas Models Compared Big Horn Outdoors

⏱12 min read
By Best Kitchen Picks Daily • September 03, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

Fall is peak pizza-oven season. The air is cool enough that standing over a 900°F stone feels good instead of punishing, and dough behaves beautifully in the low humidity. But the outdoor pizza oven market in 2026 is crowded and confusing: you can spend $180 or $1,800 and both ovens will claim "Neapolitan pizza in 60 seconds."

This comparison puts Big Horn Outdoors head-to-head against the brands people actually cross-shop it with — Ooni, Gozney, Solo Stove and Bertello — plus a look at where multi-fuel and gas-only models make sense. We've weighted the things that matter after the honeymoon period ends: how long the stone holds heat through pizza number six, how much the pellets or propane actually cost you over a season, and whether the thing survives a winter on the patio.

When evaluating Best Outdoor Pizza Ovens for Fall 2026: Top 7 Wood, the key factors to consider are build quality, long-term durability, and whether it genuinely solves the problem it claims to address.

Editor's Tip

No brand wins every category here. Big Horn is genuinely the value play, Gozney is genuinely the build-quality play, and Ooni genuinely has the best ecosystem. Below is which one fits which cook.

Big Horn Outdoors — Best Value Wood-Fired & Multi-Fuel Ovens

Big Horn Outdoors has built its reputation on undercutting the premium brands by 40–60% while delivering the same core spec sheet: a cordierite stone, stainless shell, and 900°F-plus capability. Their 12-inch pellet-fired oven typically lands in the $180–$260 range, and the larger 16-inch and multi-fuel (wood pellet + propane) models generally sit between $300 and $500 — territory where Ooni and Gozney are still selling their entry-level units. In use, the pellet models hit pizza temp in roughly 15–20 minutes and cook a 12-inch pie in 60–90 seconds with a turn. Where Big Horn cuts cost is insulation density and fit-and-finish: the shell is thinner than a Gozney Roccbox, so heat recovery between back-to-back pies is a bit slower and the exterior gets hotter to the touch. Panel gaps and hinge feel are noticeably more budget than Ooni's. But Big Horn also sells griddles, camping stoves and grills in the same catalog, and their accessory pricing (peels, covers, stone replacements, spare burners) is meaningfully cheaper than the name brands — a replacement stone that costs $50+ from a premium brand often runs half that. For someone making pizza a dozen weekends a year, that math is hard to argue with. Browse the current Big Horn Outdoors lineup here.

Ooni Karu 16 & Koda 16 — Best Ecosystem and Resale Value

Ooni is the default recommendation for a reason. The Karu 16 (around $799) is a genuine multi-fuel oven with a glass door, a built-in digital thermometer, and an optional gas burner attachment; the Koda 16 (around $599) is the gas-only sibling that's arguably the easiest oven in this list to live with — twist a knob, wait 20 minutes, cook. Ooni's L-shaped flame in the Koda 16 gives more even rear-wall heat than most single-point burners, which means less aggressive turning. The real advantage is the ecosystem: infrared thermometers, dough boxes, the Ooni app with tested recipes, a strong secondhand market, and a support operation that actually answers. The downside is straightforward — you pay roughly two to three times what a comparable Big Horn model costs, and for casual users that premium buys convenience more than better pizza.

Gozney Roccbox & Arc XL — Best Build Quality and Heat Retention

Gozney's Roccbox (about $499–$599) is the heavyweight in every sense: a dense insulated body wrapped in silicone, roughly 44 lbs for a 12-inch cooking area, and a stone that recovers heat faster than anything else in its price class. If you're cooking eight or ten pizzas for a crowd, Roccbox is where the gap between premium and budget becomes obvious — Big Horn and Solo Stove models both start losing floor temperature by pizza five or six, while the Roccbox holds. The Arc XL ($999+) steps up to a 16-inch opening with a rolled steel dome and a genuinely restaurant-grade feel. Gozney's weakness is portability and price: the Roccbox is small for its weight, and $999 for the Arc XL is a serious commitment for a patio appliance.

Solo Stove Pi Prime — Best Simple Gas-Only Option

The Pi Prime (typically $299–$399, frequently discounted) is Solo Stove's stripped-back gas oven: no wood option, no door, just a demi-dome, a stone, and a burner. It's the fastest thing here from cold to cooking — often 15 minutes — and the tapered dome does a good job pushing heat down onto the pie. It's also gorgeous in a way that matters if the oven lives on a visible patio. The tradeoffs: no wood-fired flavor at all, a 12-inch limit, and less airflow control than the multi-fuel competitors. Against a Big Horn multi-fuel model at a similar or lower price, you're trading fuel flexibility for cleaner design and easier startup.

Bertello Grande — Best Wood-Plus-Gas Simultaneous Firing

Bertello's Grande (around $399–$499) has a party trick the others don't: you can run the gas burner and the wood/charcoal tray at the same time, giving you a steady base temperature plus real wood smoke and flame. That's the closest thing to a proper Neapolitan flavor profile at this price. Build quality sits between Big Horn and Ooni — better than budget, not premium — and the included gas burner is genuinely useful rather than a $100+ upsell. Bertello's weak points are a smaller effective cooking floor than the 16-inch class and a stone that's thinner than we'd like for heavy sessions.

Fuel Costs and Season-Long Ownership

Over a fall season of roughly 15 cooking sessions, hardwood pellets run about $1–$2 per session, propane about $1.50–$3 depending on tank pricing. That's not a differentiator. What is: replacement stones and covers. Big Horn's accessory pricing is the lowest of the group; Gozney's is the highest. If you crack a stone (and you eventually will, usually from thermal shock or a wet pie), that's a $25 problem on a Big Horn and a $60–$80 problem on a Gozney or Ooni.

What Actually Makes Better Pizza

Honestly? All five of these brands can hit 850–950°F and produce excellent pizza. Beyond about $400, you're buying heat retention for volume cooking, better insulation, and nicer materials — not fundamentally better crust. Dough hydration, a 48-hour cold ferment, and learning to turn the pie every 20 seconds will improve your results far more than upgrading from a $250 oven to an $800 one.

Our Verdict

There's no single winner here — there's a winner per cook. If you make pizza a handful of weekends a year and don't want to spend $700 to find out whether you enjoy it, Big Horn Outdoors is the clear-eyed choice: you get 90% of the pizza for 40% of the money, plus multi-fuel flexibility that Solo Stove doesn't offer at the price. If you routinely feed a crowd of eight or more and want the stone to hold temperature through a dozen pies, the Gozney Roccbox or Arc XL is worth the premium — that's the one place cheap ovens genuinely fall behind. If you value ease, support and the ability to resell in two years without taking a bath, Ooni's Koda 16 remains the safest all-rounder. And if simultaneous wood-and-gas flavor is the whole point, Bertello's Grande is the specialist pick. Our practical advice: start with Big Horn if you're new, upgrade to Gozney only if you outgrow it — and check Big Horn's current pricing here before you commit to anything more expensive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a cheaper Big Horn oven really as hot as an Ooni or Gozney?

Yes — peak temperature is largely a function of burner design and dome geometry, and Big Horn models reliably hit 850–950°F just like the premium brands. The difference is heat retention and recovery, not peak heat. Premium insulated ovens like the Roccbox bounce back to temperature faster between pizzas, which only matters when you're cooking six or more in a row.

Should I buy wood-fired, gas, or multi-fuel for fall cooking?

Gas is the most forgiving in cold, windy fall weather because the burner output is constant and you're not fighting a fire. Wood and pellets give real smoke flavor but demand attention. Multi-fuel models — offered by Big Horn, Ooni's Karu line and Bertello — are the best compromise if you want gas on weeknights and wood on weekends.

Can I leave any of these ovens outside through winter?

Not uncovered. Every brand here recommends a weatherproof cover and, ideally, storing the cordierite stone indoors or at least dry, since absorbed moisture is the number one cause of cracked stones. Big Horn covers are among the cheapest available; whichever oven you buy, budget for a cover as a non-optional accessory.

How long do these ovens take to reach pizza temperature?

Gas-only models like the Solo Stove Pi Prime and Ooni Koda 16 typically take 15–20 minutes. Pellet and wood-fired ovens, including most Big Horn models, need 20–30 minutes and a few minutes more to fully saturate the stone. Always check the floor temperature with an infrared thermometer rather than trusting a dome gauge.
