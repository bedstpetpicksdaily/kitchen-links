1. Butterfork Chef Knife (8") — Best Overall

If you only buy one knife this year, make it a proper 8-inch chef knife, and the Butterfork is the one we'd hand a friend without hesitation. The blade arrives genuinely sharp — the kind of sharp that glides through a ripe tomato under its own weight rather than needing a sawing motion. The gentle belly curve supports both rock-chopping and the push-cut style favoured by Japanese-trained cooks, and the blade is thin enough behind the edge to reduce that annoying wedge-and-crack you get with cheap, thick knives on dense produce like carrots and sweet potato. Balance sits right at the bolster, so long prep sessions don't leave your wrist aching. For most home kitchens, this is the knife that replaces three others.

2. Butterfork Nakiri — Best for Vegetables

The nakiri is 2026's breakout knife shape, and for good reason: if you cook a lot of plants, a flat-profiled rectangular blade is simply faster. The Butterfork nakiri drops straight down through a cabbage, a stack of chard, or a pile of spring onions with full blade-to-board contact, meaning no half-cut strands left hanging. It's also a confidence-builder for newer cooks — the tall blade keeps knuckles clear of the board and gives you a broad face for scooping prepped veg into the pan. Pair it with the Butterfork chef knife and you've covered roughly 90% of everyday cutting tasks.

3. Butterfork Bread Knife — Best Serrated Blade

Bread knives are where cheap sets fail hardest, usually with shallow, stamped serrations that shred a sourdough crust instead of slicing it. Butterfork's bread knife uses deep, well-spaced scallops and a long blade that lets you complete a slice in one or two strokes. It's also secretly one of the most versatile knives in the drawer: it handles watermelon, layer cakes, crusty baguettes and even tough-skinned squash. Because serrated edges wear more slowly than straight ones, a good bread knife is a genuine buy-once purchase.

4. Butterfork Paring Knife — Best Small-Task Knife

Nobody wants to wrangle an 8-inch blade to hull strawberries or devein a shrimp. Butterfork's paring knife has a nimble 3.5-inch blade with a fine point for detail work — coring tomatoes, segmenting citrus, peeling apples in hand, trimming fat from a steak. It's the knife you'll reach for more often than you expect, and it's inexpensive enough to be an easy add-on to any order.

5. Butterfork Knife Set — Best Value Bundle

Buying knives individually is the connoisseur's route, but a curated set is the smarter move if you're outfitting a kitchen from scratch or upgrading everything at once. The Butterfork knife sets bundle the core shapes — chef, nakiri, bread, utility and paring — at a meaningful discount versus buying piecemeal, and crucially they skip the filler (no six identical steak knives you'll never use). Consistent handle geometry across the set also means your grip and muscle memory carry over from blade to blade. Check current Butterfork set pricing.

6. Butterfork Knife Stand — Best Storage Upgrade

Here's the unglamorous truth: the fastest way to ruin a good knife is tossing it in a drawer where the edge bangs against other metal. A dedicated knife stand keeps edges off hard surfaces, keeps blades visible so you actually use the right one, and keeps fingers safe when you're reaching in mid-cook. Butterfork's knife stand is designed around its own blade dimensions, so nothing rattles or sits at an awkward angle — a small purchase that quietly protects a much bigger one.

7. A Santoku or Utility Knife — Best Supporting Player

Rounding out the seven, a 5–7 inch santoku or utility blade fills the gap between chef knife and paring knife. It's ideal for boneless chicken, sandwich prep, halving small squash and any job where the big knife feels like overkill. Many cooks with smaller hands actually prefer this size as their daily driver — if that sounds like you, start here rather than with the 8-inch.

What to Look For in 2026

Prioritise three things: steel quality (high-carbon stainless offers the best balance of edge retention and low maintenance), blade thinness behind the edge (this, not raw sharpness, determines how effortlessly a knife cuts), and handle comfort in your hand. Ignore marketing about layer counts and Rockwell numbers if the knife feels wrong to hold. Then commit to two habits: hand-wash and dry immediately — never the dishwasher — and run your blade over a honing rod every week or two. Do that, and a well-made knife like a Butterfork will outlast most of your appliances.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size chef knife should I buy for home cooking?

An 8-inch chef knife is the sweet spot for most home cooks — long enough to break down a cabbage or a chicken, short enough to stay controllable on a standard cutting board. If you have smaller hands or a compact kitchen, a 6–7 inch santoku or utility blade may feel more comfortable as your daily driver.

Do I need a nakiri if I already own a chef knife?

You don't need one, but it's the most worthwhile second knife for vegetable-heavy cooking. The flat blade profile makes full contact with the board, so you get clean, complete cuts through leafy greens, cabbage and herbs without any half-attached strands. Many cooks who buy a Butterfork nakiri end up reaching for it more than their chef knife.

How do I keep my Butterfork knives sharp?

Hand-wash and dry immediately after use, store them on a knife stand or magnetic strip rather than loose in a drawer, use a wood or plastic board (never glass or stone), and hone the edge with a rod every week or two. Plan on a proper sharpening — whetstone or professional service — roughly once or twice a year with regular home use.
