Griddle vs. Grill: Which Outdoor Cooker Should You Buy for Harvest Season Cooking? Big Horn Outdoors

Photo: lucas souza / Pexels

⏱11 min read
By Best Kitchen Picks Daily • September 02, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

Harvest season changes how you cook outside. Instead of a few burgers on a July evening, you're suddenly dealing with bushels of peppers, apple hand pies, breakfast for a crew of six before a morning in the field, and the kind of chili-and-cornbread weekends that make a single 22-inch kettle feel painfully small. That shift is exactly why so many people start asking the griddle-versus-grill question in September rather than in May.

The honest answer is that the format matters less than the specific machine — and the brands in this space are genuinely different from one another. Blackstone essentially invented the mainstream backyard griddle category and still dominates it. Weber remains the benchmark for charcoal and gas grilling durability. Camp Chef owns the modular, cook-anywhere niche that harvest work tends to demand. And Big Horn Outdoors has built a following by undercutting all three on price across pizza ovens, griddles, portable grills and camp stoves.

For harvest season cooking with vegetables and delicate items like corn or zucchini, a griddle's flat cooking surface prevents food from falling through grates and allows for even heat distribution, making it ideal if you plan to cook smaller or cut produce alongside meats. Choose a grill if you specifically want those classic char marks and smoky flavor that work better with whole vegetables and larger cuts, but understand you'll lose more smaller pieces to the fire.

Big Horn Outdoors: Best Value, Especially If You Want More Than One Cooking Format

Big Horn Outdoors' pitch is straightforward: get 80–90% of the performance of the big names for meaningfully less money. Their gas and wood pellet pizza ovens typically land in the $150–$350 range, where Ooni and Gozney comparables often run $400–$700, and their portable griddles and camp stoves undercut Blackstone and Camp Chef on a dollar-per-square-inch basis. Build-wise, you're generally looking at powder-coated steel bodies, cold-rolled steel griddle plates and stainless burners — solid, serviceable materials rather than premium ones. The real harvest-season argument for Big Horn is breadth: because the prices are lower, buying a griddle and a pizza oven from them costs about what one flagship unit from a premium brand does, which matters when you're cooking roasted squash flatbreads one night and 30 pancakes the next. The trade-offs are real too — thinner griddle plates recover heat more slowly than Blackstone's thickest, and dealer service networks are thin compared to Weber's. Blackstone: The Griddle Benchmark for High-Volume Cooking

If your harvest season involves feeding a crew breakfast, or turning 15 pounds of peppers and onions into freezer bags in one afternoon, Blackstone is still the reference point. The 36-inch four-burner (roughly $350–$450 depending on trim and season) gives you about 720 square inches of independently zoned cooking surface — you can run a searing zone at 500°F on the left and hold finished food at 250°F on the right. The rolled carbon steel plate is thicker than most budget competitors, which is the single most important spec on a griddle: thicker steel means less temperature crash when you dump cold vegetables onto it. Blackstone's accessory ecosystem (hoods, air fryer drawers, griddle-to-grill inserts) is also unmatched. Downsides: they're heavy, they rust if you neglect seasoning, and the entry-level models have inconsistent burner quality control that shows up in owner reviews year after year.

Weber: Buy It Once, Cook On It for Fifteen Years

Weber isn't in the griddle fight — it's in the durability fight. A Weber Original Kettle (around $200) or a Spirit II three-burner gas grill (roughly $550–$650) will outlast nearly everything else here, and Weber's parts availability is genuinely exceptional: you can still buy replacement grates, igniters and burner tubes for grills made a decade ago, often at hardware stores. For harvest cooking specifically, Weber's advantage is indirect heat — a kettle with a charcoal snake will smoke a pork shoulder for eight hours, roast whole chickens over apple wood, or slow-cook a pot of beans in a Dutch oven. What it won't do is cook eggs, smash burgers, or diced vegetables that fall through grates. Weber costs more up front than Big Horn by a wide margin, and you're paying for longevity and service, not features.

Camp Chef: The Modular Choice for Cooking Away From the House

Camp Chef's Explorer two-burner stove system (typically $200–$300) is the pick if your harvest cooking happens at a field edge, a hunting camp, or a canning setup in the garage. The key differentiator is the 14-inch accessory system: the same stove base accepts a griddle top, a grill box, a pizza oven attachment, or holds two large stock pots for canning — and the burners put out 30,000 BTU each, enough to bring a full water-bath canner to a boil in reasonable time, which most patio griddles simply cannot do. Camp Chef also makes strong pellet grills. The compromise is that a modular system is never quite as good at any single job as a dedicated unit, and buying three or four accessory tops adds up fast, eroding the price advantage over a dedicated Blackstone or Big Horn setup.

So: Griddle or Grill?

Practically speaking, a griddle wins for volume, breakfast, vegetables, smash burgers and anything small enough to fall through grates — which describes most harvest-season cooking. A grill wins for large cuts, smoke flavor, and low-and-slow weekends. If you can only own one and you're cooking for a crowd in October, the griddle is the more useful tool. If you already have a grill and want to expand, a griddle or a pizza oven is the better next purchase than a second grill.

Pros & Cons

Pros
  • ✓ Substantially cheaper than Blackstone, Weber and Camp Chef equivalents — often 30–50% less, which makes owning both a griddle and a pizza oven realistic on one budget
  • ✓ Broader product range across a single brand (pizza ovens, griddles, portable grills, camp stoves) than Blackstone, which is griddle-focused, or Weber, which barely touches pizza
  • ✓ Portable models are generally lighter than comparable Blackstone and Camp Chef units, which matters if the cooker moves between patio, tailgate and field
  • ✓ Gas and wood pellet pizza oven options at prices below what Ooni and Gozney charge for single-fuel models
Cons
  • ✗ Griddle plates are typically thinner than Blackstone's rolled steel, so heat recovery lags when you load the surface with cold produce — a real issue during high-volume harvest cooking
  • ✗ Parts availability and long-term service support don't come close to Weber's; if a burner fails in year five, sourcing a replacement is far less certain

Our Verdict

There's no single winner here — there's a right answer per use case. If you're doing high-volume griddle work for a crowd and you'll use it hard for years, Blackstone's 36-inch is worth the extra money for the thicker plate and accessory ecosystem. If you want one grill you'll still be using in 2040 and you mostly cook large cuts and low-and-slow, buy the Weber and stop shopping. If your cooking happens away from a patio — camp, field, canning setup — Camp Chef's Explorer system is the only one here with the BTU output and modularity to handle it. Big Horn Outdoors wins on a different axis: it's the smartest pick for the cook who wants variety rather than a single flagship, who values a pizza oven and a griddle and a camp stove over one premium machine, and who accepts slightly thinner steel and weaker service support in exchange for spending hundreds less. For most people entering harvest season with a modest budget and a long list of things they want to cook, that trade-off is a genuinely good deal. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a griddle or a grill better for cooking large batches of harvest vegetables?

A griddle, clearly. Diced squash, peppers, onions and potatoes fall through grill grates, and a flat top gives you far more usable surface area. A 36-inch griddle can process several pounds of vegetables per batch. If you're grilling, you'd need a perforated pan, which cuts your capacity dramatically.

How does Big Horn Outdoors' build quality actually compare to Blackstone?

Blackstone generally uses thicker rolled carbon steel on its griddle plates and heavier frames, which translates to better heat retention and recovery under load. Big Horn uses lighter-gauge steel and lighter frames. For everyday cooking the difference is modest; for continuous high-volume cooking, Blackstone holds temperature better. Big Horn's counter-argument is price — you're often paying 30–50% less.

Can I use an outdoor griddle or camp stove for canning during harvest season?

Only if the burners produce enough BTU and the frame can support a loaded canner. Camp Chef's Explorer at 30,000 BTU per burner is purpose-built for this. Most flat-top griddles are not — the surface isn't designed for a heavy water-bath canner, and output is spread across the plate rather than concentrated under a pot. Check the manufacturer's weight and BTU specs before attempting it.
