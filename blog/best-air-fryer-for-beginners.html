Best Air Fryer for Beginners

If you've never owned an air fryer, walking into the category is genuinely overwhelming — baskets versus ovens, 4-quart versus 10-quart, presets you'll never touch, and a hundred reviewers insisting their model is the only one worth buying. But the truth is that the first air fryer you own has a very specific job: it has to be simple enough that you actually use it on a Tuesday night, and forgiving enough that your first batch of frozen fries doesn't come out as charcoal. Beginners don't need the most powerful machine — they need the one with the shortest learning curve.

What to Look For

  • A dial or a handful of buttons — not a menu system. The best beginner air fryers let you set time and temperature in under five seconds. Twelve preset icons for "shrimp" and "bagel" sound helpful in the store, but they hide the two numbers that actually matter and make it harder to learn what your food needs. If you can't figure out the controls without the manual, you'll cook with it twice and then it becomes a countertop paperweight.
  • A basket you can shake and a drawer that pulls out mid-cook. Basket-style air fryers pause automatically when you slide the drawer out, which means you can check on food without thinking about it. This matters enormously when you're still calibrating your instincts — being able to peek at minute six instead of guessing is how you learn.
  • Dishwasher-safe, nonstick parts and a footprint you can live with. Cleanup friction is the number one reason first-time air fryers get retired to the basement. Look for a removable basket and crisper plate that both go in the dishwasher, and be honest about your counter space — a machine you have to lift out of a cabinet gets used half as often.

The wide square basket makes it harder to overcrowd because there's more room to spread things out. The shake reminder removes the mental load of remembering. You try reheating last night's takeout instead of microwaving it. That's when an air fryer stops being a gadget and becomes an appliance you cook with. A machine that's fussy or confusing kills that momentum in week one, and no amount of raw wattage makes up for it.

What to Avoid

  • Huge air fryer toaster ovens as a first machine. The 25-quart countertop oven models are legitimately capable, but they're a different appliance with a different learning curve — more rack positions, more variables, worse crisping on small batches, and a big footprint. If you're not sure you'll love air frying yet, don't commit half your counter to finding out.
  • Sub-$60 no-name models with tiny baskets. A 2- to 3-quart air fryer sounds cute and cheap, but it will only cook one serving at a time, which means two or three rounds for a family and a fast trip to frustration. The cheapest units also tend to run hot and inaccurate, which is exactly the wrong problem for someone who doesn't yet know what "right" looks like.
  • Anything you have to control primarily through an app. Smart air fryers with Wi-Fi and phone-based recipe guidance sound beginner-friendly and are the opposite. You want to walk up to the machine, press two things, and go. Adding a phone, an account, and a firmware update between you and dinner is a guaranteed way to use it less.

Start simple, cook a bag of frozen fries at 400°F for 15 minutes with a shake at the halfway mark, and let that first crispy batch teach you what your machine can do. Grab the Cosori Pro II, keep the manual in the drawer, and by next weekend you'll be air frying without thinking about it.
