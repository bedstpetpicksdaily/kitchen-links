Gas vs. Wood-Fired Pizza Ovens: Which Should You Buy in 2026? Big Horn Outdoors

By Best Kitchen Picks Daily • September 03, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

The gas-versus-wood debate used to be simple: wood tasted better, gas was easier. In 2026 that line has blurred. Gas burners now hit 900°F in 15 minutes, wood-fired chambers have gotten smarter about airflow, and almost every major brand sells a dual-fuel model that tries to have it both ways. The real question isn't "which fuel is better" — it's "which fuel matches how you actually cook, and which brand builds the best version of it at your budget."

To answer that, we compared four brands that dominate the backyard pizza oven market right now: Big Horn Outdoors, Ooni, Gozney, and Solo Stove. These aren't interchangeable. They sit at genuinely different price points, use different insulation strategies, and reward different types of cooks. One of them makes a great first oven for someone who isn't sure they'll use it every weekend. Another one is a small commercial appliance in disguise.

Below is an honest breakdown of where each brand wins, where it compromises, and what fuel type you should be buying in 2026 based on your patio, your patience, and your wallet.

First: Gas vs. Wood — What Actually Changes

Gas (propane) gets you from cold to 850–950°F in roughly 15–20 minutes with a knob, holds temperature between pizzas without babysitting, and produces essentially zero flavor of its own. Wood — usually kiln-dried hardwood or hardwood pellets — takes 20–35 minutes, requires you to feed the fire every 3–5 minutes during a cook, and does contribute a faint smoke character, though far less than people expect at 90-second bake times. The bigger practical difference is recovery: gas rebuilds stone temperature faster between back-to-back pizzas, which matters enormously if you're feeding eight people. Wood is more fun, more variable, and more work. If you plan to cook pizza more than twice a month for a crowd, gas or dual-fuel is the pragmatic answer.

Big Horn Outdoors — Best Value, Especially for Dual-Fuel Beginners

Big Horn Outdoors has built its reputation on undercutting the premium brands by a wide margin while still delivering the fundamentals: a cordierite stone, a stainless steel chamber, and models that run on wood pellets, wood chunks, charcoal, or propane depending on the configuration. Their portable pellet ovens typically land in the roughly $130–$250 range, and their larger gas and dual-fuel units generally sit around $250–$400 — often less than half what a comparable Gozney costs. The trade-off is insulation and thermal mass: the thinner-walled Big Horn units lose heat faster in wind and cold, so a 45°F evening will noticeably slow your recovery between pies. What you get in exchange is genuine flexibility and a low enough entry price that buying one isn't a commitment. For someone who wants to try wood-fired cooking without spending $700 to find out whether they like it, this is the most rational purchase on the list. Ooni — The Ecosystem Play

Ooni is the category's default recommendation for a reason. The Koda 16 (around $599) is a gas-only oven with an L-shaped burner that spreads flame across the back and one side, which meaningfully reduces the amount of turning you have to do mid-bake. The Karu 12G (around $399) is the multi-fuel option, and the Ooni Volt 12 (around $999) is the electric one for people with balconies and HOA rules. Ooni's real advantage isn't any single oven — it's the accessory ecosystem, the app-connected thermometers, the enormous body of recipes and community troubleshooting, and a customer service operation that actually replaces failed igniters. Compared to Big Horn, you pay roughly a 60–100% premium for better insulation, better flame geometry, and a support network. Compared to Gozney, Ooni feels lighter-duty and more consumer-oriented.

Gozney — The Premium Wood and Dual-Fuel Choice

Gozney builds the heaviest, most thermally stable ovens in the consumer market. The Roccbox (around $499) is a genuinely portable oven with a silicone-jacketed exterior and swappable gas and wood burners — it's the one professional chefs tend to own for home use. The Arc XL (around $1,299) has a wide 16-inch mouth, a rolled steel dome, and enough stone mass that it holds temperature through a dozen pizzas without sagging. That thermal mass is the whole argument for Gozney: it's the difference between pizza #1 and pizza #10 tasting the same. The downsides are weight (the Arc XL is not something you carry to a campsite) and price. If you're comparing a Gozney Arc XL to a Big Horn dual-fuel unit, you're comparing a $1,300 appliance to a $300 one, and the performance gap is real — just not 4x real for most casual users.

Solo Stove Pi Prime — The Simplest Gas Option

The Pi Prime (around $399, frequently discounted) is gas-only, deliberately. Solo Stove stripped out the multi-fuel complexity and built a demi-dome oven with a clean burner and a fast preheat. It's the easiest oven here to hand to a guest without a tutorial. But it's also the least flexible: no wood, no charcoal, no smoke character, and a smaller effective cooking area than the Koda 16. If you know for certain you'll never want to burn wood, this is a clean, well-made choice. If there's any chance you'll want to, Big Horn's dual-fuel units cost less and Gozney's Roccbox does both better.

Price and Fuel at a Glance

Big Horn Outdoors: ~$130–$400, wood pellet / charcoal / gas / dual-fuel. Ooni: ~$399–$999, gas, multi-fuel, or electric. Gozney: ~$499–$1,299+, gas and wood via swappable burners. Solo Stove Pi Prime: ~$399, gas only. Prices shift with seasonal promotions, so verify before buying.

Our Verdict

There's no single winner here — there's a right answer per buyer. Buy Big Horn Outdoors if you're new to outdoor pizza, want dual-fuel flexibility, or simply don't want to spend $600+ to find out whether you'll use this thing after August; it delivers 80% of the pizza at 40% of the price. Buy Ooni — specifically the Koda 16 — if you want the safest all-round gas oven with the deepest accessory ecosystem and the best odds of getting a replacement part in two years. Buy Gozney if you're going to cook for crowds regularly and you care about pizza #10 tasting identical to pizza #1; the Arc XL's thermal mass genuinely can't be matched at lower price points, and the Roccbox is the best portable wood-and-gas unit made. Buy the Solo Stove Pi Prime only if you're certain you want gas and nothing else. On fuel: for 2026, we'd steer most buyers toward dual-fuel or gas — wood is more romantic, but gas is what actually gets used on a Tuesday.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does wood-fired pizza actually taste different from gas?

Slightly, but less than most people expect. At 900°F a Neapolitan pizza bakes in 60–90 seconds, which isn't long enough to absorb much smoke. The bigger flavor difference comes from stone temperature and leoparding on the crust, which both gas and wood can produce. Wood contributes a faint char-and-smoke note that's most noticeable on longer bakes and on non-pizza cooks like roasted vegetables or bread.

Is a cheaper Big Horn oven a false economy compared to an Ooni or Gozney?

Not necessarily — it depends on volume. If you cook two or three pizzas a few times a summer, the insulation gap barely shows up. If you're doing pizza parties for ten people, the faster heat recovery of a Gozney Arc XL or Ooni Koda 16 will save you real frustration, and the price premium starts to justify itself.

Can I use a propane pizza oven on a covered patio or balcony?

Propane ovens must be used outdoors with clearance overhead and on all sides — check your specific model's manual and your local fire code, as many apartment complexes ban propane on balconies entirely. If that's your situation, an electric model like the Ooni Volt 12 is the only realistic option among these brands.
