Tormek T-8 vs T-4 vs T-2: Which Wet Sharpening System Should You Buy in 2026? Tormek

Photo: Daniel & Hannah Snipes / Pexels

⏱12 min read
By Best Kitchen Picks Daily • September 03, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

Wet sharpening systems occupy a strange spot in the tool world: they cost more than most people expect, they work more slowly than a belt grinder, and yet chefs, carvers and cabinetmakers keep buying them. The reason is simple — a slow-turning stone flooded with water cannot burn a temper or blue an edge, so you can grind a chisel or a Japanese gyuto down to a mirror bevel without ever wondering whether you just ruined the steel.

Tormek is the Swedish brand that essentially invented this category, and in 2026 it sells three very different machines: the flagship T-8 (and its T-8 Custom / Black variants), the compact T-4, and the fully automated T-2 Pro kitchen knife machine. But Tormek isn't the only name in the aisle. Tormek's own lineup competes directly against the Jet JSSG-10, the budget Grizzly T10010ANV, the Scheppach Tiger series, and — from an entirely different direction — the Work Sharp Ken Onion Edition belt sharpener and the Edge Pro Apex manual system.

When evaluating Tormek T, the key factors to consider are build quality, long-term durability, and whether it genuinely solves the problem it claims to address.

Editor's Tip

This comparison looks at what each machine actually does differently: motor duty cycle, stone quality, jig ecosystem, price per year of use, and how long it takes to put a working edge on a knife. There is no single winner here, and we'll say plainly which machine loses to which competitor and why.

Tormek T-8 — the reference standard (around $850–$900)

The T-8 is the machine everyone else is measured against. It runs a 200mm SG-250 grindstone at roughly 90 rpm on a fully sealed, zinc-cast EzyLock shaft, driven by an industrial AC motor rated for continuous duty — you can sharpen for an hour straight without a cooldown break. The two things that justify the price are the Universal Support with micro-adjust, which lets you dial bevel angles in fractions of a degree and return to them repeatably, and the ~40-strong jig ecosystem covering chisels, plane irons, gouges, turning tools, axes, scissors, drill bits and knives. The included Anglemaster WM-200 plus the marker-and-cut method gets you a repeatable 15° or 20° per side within about a minute. The leather honing wheel takes the edge past the stone's 1000-grit finish to something genuinely push-cutting sharp. Downsides: it's heavy at ~14.5 kg, the water trough drips if you overfill it, and the SG stone dishes over time, so you'll eventually buy a TT-50 truing tool ($100+) if it didn't come in your kit.

Tormek T-4 — the same geometry in a smaller box (around $520–$600)

The T-4 uses a 200mm-diameter stone as well but a narrower one, in a plastic housing with a smaller motor rated for roughly 30 minutes of continuous use before it wants a rest. Critically, it accepts the same Universal Support and the same jigs as the T-8, so if you're sharpening kitchen knives and a modest set of chisels, the sharpening results are functionally identical to the flagship. What you give up is duty cycle, stone width (less room for wide plane irons), the micro-adjust on the support bar (available separately), and long-term rebuildability. At roughly $300 less than the T-8, the T-4 is the correct choice for most home users — and honestly, most people who buy a T-8 for kitchen knives alone have overbought.

Tormek T-2 Pro — the automated kitchen machine (around $1,000+)

The T-2 is not a hobbyist tool and shouldn't be compared to the others on price. It's a diamond-wheel, composite-honing-wheel machine designed for restaurant and retail environments where a non-expert needs to sharpen a knife in about 30 seconds with no skill and no water tray to maintain. It sets a fixed 15° edge, it's fast, and it's consistent. But it does one thing only: knives. If you own a workshop, it's the wrong machine entirely. If you run a kitchen with twelve line cooks and you're paying a mobile sharpening service $200 a month, it pays for itself inside a year.

Jet JSSG-10 — the closest direct rival (around $600–$700)

Jet's wet sharpener is the most credible non-Tormek machine in this format. It uses a larger 10-inch stone, an 8-inch leather wheel and a robust cast base, and Tormek-pattern jigs will fit its support bar in most configurations — a fact plenty of owners exploit by buying a Jet body and Tormek jigs. Where the Jet loses is refinement: the support bar adjustment is coarser, the stone quality is generally rated a step below Tormek's SG grade, and the stone-truing solution is less elegant. Where it wins is water management and price — you get a bigger stone for less than a T-8. For a hobby woodworker who values grinding capacity over micro-repeatability, the Jet is a legitimately smart buy.

Grizzly T10010ANV and Scheppach Tiger 2500 — the budget clones ($200–$300)

These are the machines that make people question Tormek's pricing. The Grizzly T10010ANV and Scheppach Tiger 2500 both replicate the wet-wheel-plus-leather-strop layout for roughly a quarter of the T-8's price, and both will absolutely put a shaving edge on a chisel. The real differences show up in the details: cruder stone grit consistency, more runout on the shaft, plastic support bars that flex under jig pressure, and motors that get hot after 15–20 minutes. Repeatability suffers — you can hit an angle, but hitting the same angle next month is harder. For someone sharpening a dozen tools a year, that's a non-issue and the savings are enormous. For a pro who resharpens daily, the flex and runout become a daily irritation.

Work Sharp Ken Onion Edition + Blade Grinding Attachment (around $150 + $100)

Different technology, same job. The Work Sharp is a variable-speed belt sharpener that will reprofile a badly damaged knife in about 90 seconds — something a Tormek needs ten patient minutes to do. It's dramatically cheaper, far more portable, and honestly more fun to use. The trade-off is heat and material removal: belts run dry and hot, they eat steel fast, and an inattentive user can round a tip or over-thin a blade permanently. It's excellent for pocket knives, hunting knives and beaters; it's the wrong tool for a $400 Japanese single-bevel or a set of vintage chisels you care about.

Edge Pro Apex 4 (around $275)

The manual alternative. A guided-rod system with graded water stones, no motor, no heat, total control. Apex users routinely produce edges finer than anything off a Tormek honing wheel, because you can go all the way to 3000+ grit and strop by hand. What it can't do is bulk removal or woodworking tools — chisels, gouges and axes are out of scope, and a full reprofile on a thick blade takes real time and elbow grease. For a knife obsessive who owns no chisels, an Edge Pro plus a good strop beats a T-4 on results per dollar.

Pros & Cons

Pros
  • ✓ Repeatability the budget clones can't match: the T-8's micro-adjust Universal Support returns to the exact same bevel angle months later, where Grizzly and Scheppach plastic bars flex and drift under jig load
  • ✓ Continuous-duty motor — the T-8 sharpens for an hour straight, while the Grizzly T10010ANV and Scheppach Tiger want a rest after 15–20 minutes
  • ✓ No heat risk at ~90 rpm under water, unlike the Work Sharp Ken Onion belt system, which can blue a tip or over-thin a blade in seconds if you're careless
  • ✓ The broadest jig ecosystem in the category (40+ jigs for gouges, axes, scissors, drill bits), where Edge Pro handles knives only and Jet relies partly on Tormek-pattern accessories anyway
Cons
  • ✗ Cost per result is hard to defend for casual users — a Scheppach Tiger 2500 at roughly a quarter the price puts a shaving edge on a chisel too, just less repeatably
  • ✗ Slow for heavy reprofiling: a Work Sharp Ken Onion fixes a chipped, badly worn knife in about 90 seconds versus 10+ minutes on a T-8, and Tormek accessories (TT-50 truing tool, extra jigs) add $100–$300 to the real cost

Our Verdict

There's no universal winner here — pick by use case. If you sharpen kitchen knives and a handful of chisels at home, the Tormek T-4 is the sweet spot: identical jig system, identical results, roughly $300 less than the T-8, and its shorter duty cycle simply won't matter to you. If you're a professional woodworker or run a sharpening side business, the T-8's continuous-duty motor, sealed shaft and micro-adjust support are worth the premium and it will outlive two or three budget machines. If you mostly grind wide plane irons and care more about stone size than fractional-degree repeatability, the Jet JSSG-10 gives you more wheel for less money and takes Tormek jigs anyway. If your total tool sharpening is a dozen items a year, buy the Scheppach Tiger 2500 or Grizzly T10010ANV and put the $600 you saved into better chisels — you will not regret it. If you only sharpen knives and want the finest possible edge, the Edge Pro Apex 4 beats every powered machine on this list for pure edge quality. And if you need to fix beat-up outdoor knives fast, the Work Sharp Ken Onion is a better tool than any Tormek at a fifth of the price. Buy the Tormek when repeatability and tool variety matter more than speed or budget — you can browse the current T-4, T-8 and T-2 configurations on Tormek's official site.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Tormek jigs fit the Jet JSSG-10 and the budget clones?

Mostly yes for the Jet — its 12mm support bar takes Tormek-pattern jigs in most configurations, which is why many owners pair a Jet body with Tormek accessories. Grizzly and Scheppach machines also use 12mm bars, but bar flex and shaft runout mean the same jig won't deliver the same repeatability it does on a T-8.

Is the T-8 worth $300 more than the T-4 for home use?

For most home users, no. The T-4 uses the same jigs and produces the same edge geometry. The T-8 earns its premium on duty cycle (continuous vs. roughly 30 minutes), stone width for wide plane irons, the sealed metal shaft and long-term rebuildability. Sharpen daily and it's worth it; sharpen monthly and it isn't.

Which is faster on a badly damaged knife — Tormek or Work Sharp?

Work Sharp, by a wide margin. A Ken Onion Edition with the Blade Grinding Attachment reprofiles a chipped edge in a minute or two; a Tormek stone at 90 rpm takes ten minutes or more. The trade-off is that the belt runs hot and removes steel fast, so on expensive or thin blades the Tormek's slowness is a safety feature, not a flaw.
